## Context

On 2026-09-21 the founder named civilian deaths as a subject the project
should eventually track, and deferred it (README.md, open decision 5). On
2026-09-24 the founder took it up, widened it to "all war crimes of Russia in
Ukraine", and added a second project: a website kept up to date as a
**memorial**.

This is the most personal-data-sensitive subject area the project has
considered. It involves named individuals, the cause and circumstances of
their death, and their surviving relatives, many of them in occupied
territory. The governing texts already reach it:

- **POL-0001 §5.5** (victims: preserve yes; structure only for a
  documentation or accountability purpose; publish under §8.1).
- **POL-0001 §5.9** (deceased persons: outside the GDPR by Recital 27, inside
  the project's dignity standard; family interests weighed).
- **POL-0001 §8.1** (the *already-public standard*: the project never becomes
  the first publisher of a victim's name).
- **DR-0071(b)/(c)** (no automatic structuring of personal data; no at-scale
  submission intake until the quarantine and confidential-identity machinery
  is operational and tested).
- **DR-0072**, the founder's 2026-09-08 ruling and **DR-0101** (no collection
  at scale until the POL-0001 §10 review is recorded, and "recorded" means
  one specific act).
- **DR-0106** already names the "effect" half of the strike-tracking goal,
  and civilian-casualty data specifically, as the parts most exposed to the
  still-pending review.

Separately, the A6 legal-review brief's drafting found that LIL Art. 46
limits processing of criminal-offence data to a closed list this project
may not sit in. That puts POL-0001 §8.3's legal posture in question, and
names of victims of alleged crimes are exactly where it bites. This record
does not resolve that; see Consequence 2.

## Alternatives considered

Each question was put with three or four named options. The option chosen
is marked; the others are summarised with the reason they were not
recommended.

1. **How the memorial relates to the archive.** (A) the memorial is simply
   the archive's public face, one project; (B) two separate projects, the
   memorial with its own database and rules; **(C) shared evidence, separate
   memorial — chosen.** B would have been fastest but gives two personal-data
   regimes, drift between the two, and memorial entries with no evidence
   behind them. A ties the memorial's voice and pace entirely to the
   archive's.
2. **What the archive tracks first.** (A) civilian deaths only; (B) every
   crime category at once; **(C) a model built for every category, filled
   with civilian deaths first — chosen.** A would need rework when other
   categories arrive. B pulls the most dangerous categories (deported
   children, sexual violence) in by default.
3. **Who the memorial remembers.** (A) civilians only; (B) civilians and all
   Ukrainian military dead; **(C) civilians plus soldiers who died as victims
   of war crimes — chosen.** A leaves out executed prisoners of war, among
   the best-documented crimes of the war. B builds a military casualty list,
   carries operational-security and family-safety risk, duplicates state
   commemoration, and mixes combat deaths into a project about crimes.
4. **What is recorded.** (A) incidents and counts only; (B) named
   individuals as the basic unit; **(C) both, in layers — chosen.** A
   cannot name anyone. B loses the unnamed dead and is the most
   personal-data-heavy design from day one.
5. **Which sources first.** (A) official counts: UN monitoring and the
   Prosecutor General; (B) regional administration Telegram channels; (C)
   named-victim sources and existing memorials. **Chosen: A, then B, then C,
   each step a separate decision.**
6. **Family participation.** (A) none beyond a correction/removal contact;
   (B) an open public submission form; **(C) moderated contributions
   handled like witness material — chosen.** A leaves families unable to
   consent to naming. B maximises false entries, disinformation and the
   store of data on vulnerable living people.
7. **When the memorial goes live.** (A) nothing public until both parts of
   the legal brief are answered; (B) launch when built, with names from
   already-public sources; **(C) staged, each stage with its own trigger —
   chosen.** A leaves no public presence for an open-ended period even for
   material without personal data. B publishes names before counsel answers
   the question counsel was asked.
8. **Hosting.** (A) on the archive's own server; **(B) a separate static
   site on its own EU hosting — chosen;** (C) a managed website platform. A
   exposes the archive to every attack on the memorial. C puts the
   memorial's content outside the archive's review steps, contradicting
   Decision 1, and is usually US-hosted.
9. **Languages.** (A) Ukrainian only; **(B) Ukrainian first, English second
   — chosen;** (C) Ukrainian, English and French; (D) Ukrainian, English and
   Russian. C and D are left open as later, separate decisions, not
   rejected.
10. **Web address.** (A) a sub-address of the archive's domain; **(B) its
    own address with an EU registrar — chosen;** (C) a Ukrainian `.ua`
    address. C may be added later as a redirect alongside B.

## Decision

1. **Shared evidence, separate memorial.** The archive holds the sources and
   the facts about each death — who, when, where, how — each tied to its
   evidence. The memorial is a separate website with its own voice and
   design, suited to remembrance rather than research. **Every factual
   detail the memorial shows points to an archive record.** Anything a
   family submits enters the archive first, as a source (Decision 6). The
   memorial is a publication surface of the archive in the sense of
   Principle 18 ("archive first, website last"); it adds no facts of its
   own.

2. **Built for every crime category, civilian deaths filled first.** The
   archive's model for this area is built around *a documented incident, the
   type of crime alleged, and the harm it caused*. A death is one kind of
   harm, alongside injury, destruction, deportation or detention. The order
   of filling:
   - **first:** civilian deaths;
   - **next:** attacks on hospitals, schools and power infrastructure, which
     carry little personal data and overlap the strike tracking of DR-0106;
   - **each by its own later decision, with stricter rules:** deportation of
     children (POL-0001 §5.7), sexual violence (special-category data;
     survivors and witnesses under §5.6), torture and illegal detention.

   The exact data model is **not** decided here; it is the subject of a
   working paper (Consequence 5).

3. **Who the memorial remembers:** civilians killed as a result of Russia's
   war — in strikes, under occupation, in detention, in deportation — **and
   soldiers who died as victims of war crimes**: executed prisoners of war,
   soldiers killed after surrendering, deaths from torture or neglect in
   captivity. Combat deaths are not included; including them would be its
   own later decision. Remembrance does not assert a legal verdict. The
   archive records whether a death is alleged to be a crime, under
   investigation, or established by a court (Phase I record §62–63), and the
   memorial shows that status honestly without it deciding who is
   remembered. For prisoners of war, **Geneva Convention III Art. 13's
   protection against public curiosity** governs imagery and detail, even
   after death (POL-0001 §8.2).

4. **Incidents and people, in layers.**
   1. **The incident is the foundation.** Each is recorded with each
      source's own count of killed and injured, children separately. Counts
      from different sources are never averaged or merged into one figure;
      each stays that source's claim (DR-0030). The unnamed dead are always
      counted.
   2. **A person is added only when a source names them**, by a reviewer
      creating the entry by hand, never automatically from extracted data
      (DR-0071(b), POL-0001 §4, DR-0066). Entries are held at a restricted
      access tier (DR-0086) and linked to their incident.
   3. **The memorial shows both.** Every incident has its own page; a person
      appears by name only under the already-public standard (POL-0001
      §8.1).

5. **Sources in three steps, each a separate decision.**
   1. UN monitoring (the UN human-rights monitoring mission in Ukraine) and
      Ukraine's Prosecutor General — counts, with almost no names;
   2. regional administration (oblast) Telegram channels — per-incident
      reports;
   3. named-victim sources and existing memorial initiatives, approached as
      potential partners, not only as sources.

   **Each source within each step is still verified live and registered one
   at a time, by the founder, as every source so far has been** (DR-0067,
   DR-0093 §3). This record registers nothing. Step 2 is a larger scale-up
   (around two dozen channels) and is weighed against the suspension of
   collection at scale when it is put to the founder.

6. **Moderated family contributions, handled like witness material.**
   - **Removals are fast; additions are slow.** A request to remove or hide
     a name is acted on first and reviewed afterwards, because a wrongful
     naming does lasting harm and a few days' hiding costs little. Requests
     are logged and answered with a recorded rationale (POL-0001 §6).
   - **A submission is a source.** It is preserved in the archive through
     quarantine (DR-0069). The submitter's identity is held in the separate
     confidential store under a pseudonymous ID (POL-0001 §5.6), not in the
     research data.
   - **Nothing appears without a person's review.** A family's consent to
     naming counts only when another source confirms the death. Remembrance
     texts and photographs are published only with explicit consent.
   - **A correction and removal contact exists from the memorial's first
     public day.** Submission intake opens only when both Decision 7 stage 4
     conditions hold.

7. **The memorial goes live in stages, each with its own trigger.**
   1. **Now:** design and private build. Nothing public.
   2. **When the archive holds verified material from step 1 sources:** a
      first public version of incident pages — counts, places, dates — with
      **no names and no personal details**, each page approved by a person
      (Gate 3, DR-0066). Facts and figures are cited with attribution;
      reuse of others' text or photographs waits for Part B of the legal
      brief (copyright and media counsel). The site says plainly that names
      are coming, and why.
   3. **Names**, only after the Decision Record superseding DR-0072 is
      approved (DR-0101's recording act), and then only under POL-0001
      §8.1.
   4. **Family submissions**, only after stage 3 **and** once the quarantine
      and confidential-identity machinery is operational and tested
      (DR-0071(c), POL-0001 §9(c)).

8. **Hosting: a separate static site on its own EU hosting.** The archive
   generates the memorial as pre-built static pages from approved, published
   records only, and pushes them to a separate hosting package with IONOS in
   Spain — the same provider and country as the archive (DR-0100), so no
   change of hosting country arises. **Nothing flows back from the memorial
   to the archive**, and the memorial has no database. A traffic-flood
   protection service sits in front of it. Before stage 3 (names), either
   that service is EU-based or the choice goes to counsel, since the common
   services are US-based and names would raise a GDPR Chapter V transfer
   question. Family submissions (stage 4) get their own secure intake, not
   running on the memorial site.

9. **Languages: Ukrainian first, English second.** Ukrainian is the
   original text; English follows. Remembrance texts are translated only
   with a person's review, never published straight from machine
   translation, and a lag between the two versions is marked. **Names use
   the Ukrainian form as the reference**, with Ukraine's official Latin
   transliteration in English; other spellings (Russian-derived, variant
   English) are kept for search only. The archive stores every text with
   its language from the start. French, Russian or any other language is
   each a later, separate decision.

10. **Web address: the memorial's own domain, from an EU registrar.** The
    name, suited to remembrance and workable in Ukrainian and English, is
    the founder's to choose and is **still open**. The obvious variants are
    registered defensively against fake copies. The registrant is the
    founder, with public lookup details hidden, until the association
    (DR-0104) legally exists; the transfer is part of the same handover as
    the controller role. Each memorial page states it is built on the
    archive's evidence and links to it.

## Consequences

1. **This record authorises no collection, registration, structuring or
   publication.** It sets direction and order. Every source registration,
   the first Gate 2 structuring of a person, and each Decision 7 stage
   remains its own act. Approval of this record is not approval of any of
   them.

2. **The legal posture stays open, and this area depends on it most.** The
   question POL-0001 §8.3 and LIL Arts. 46/80 raise is exactly the one this
   area's naming stage turns on. Nothing here restates §8.3 as settled.
   Stage 3 (names) is tied to the DR-0101 recording act for that reason. If
   counsel's advice narrows what may be named, Decisions 3, 4 and 7 are
   revisited by supersession.

3. **Named review triggers** (in the sense POL-0001 §11 and DR-0101 use):
   - the Decision Record superseding DR-0072 → opens stage 3, and requires
     re-reading Decisions 3, 4, 6 and 7 against the advice;
   - Part B of the legal brief answered → governs reuse of third-party text
     and photographs on the memorial;
   - quarantine and confidential-identity machinery tested → with stage 3,
     opens stage 4;
   - the association's legal formation → registrant and controller handover
     (DR-0104).

4. **Security is a design requirement, not an afterthought.** The memorial
   is expected to be attacked. The separation in Decision 8 protects the
   archive; the defensive domains in Decision 10 protect families from fake
   copies; the intake in Decision 6 protects submitters. A security review
   of the memorial is expected before its first public version.

5. **Next design work: a working paper on the incident / harm / person
   model**, including how it relates to the existing assertion and evidence
   layers, how DR-0030 counts are held per source, how a person is linked
   to an incident, how duplicate persons across sources and scripts are
   reconciled by a human, and which crime-type vocabulary is used. That
   paper carries its own candidate records; this record decides only the
   shape stated in Decisions 2 and 4.

6. **Next acquisition work: live verification of the step 1 sources**, one
   at a time, presented to the founder as candidates in the usual way.

7. **Next memorial work:** the private design (stage 1) and the founder's
   choice of domain name. Neither publishes anything.

8. **README.md open decision 5** becomes "started — governed by this
   record"; **CLAUDE.md** gains a standing-rulings entry so no session
   reopens these ten questions without the founder.
